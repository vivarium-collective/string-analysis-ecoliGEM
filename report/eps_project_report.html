EPS-Producing E. coli GEM — Project Report

Project: string-analysis-ecoliGEM  |  Model: iML1515  |  Date: May 2026

1. What Is This Project?

We are using a genome-scale metabolic model (GEM) of E. coli to simulate and predict extracellular polysaccharide (EPS) production. The goal is to build a simulated EPS-forming cell that we can use to estimate how much EPS a cell could produce under different conditions — giving quantitative predictions to guide lab experiments.

What is a GEM / FBA?
A GEM is a mathematical model of all the chemical reactions in a cell. Flux Balance Analysis (FBA) finds the set of reaction rates (fluxes) that keeps all metabolites at steady state while maximising a chosen objective (e.g. growth or EPS production). Outputs are in units of mmol per gram dry weight per hour (mmol/gDW/h), which can be converted to per-cell amounts using the E. coli dry weight (~280 fg/cell).

The model used is iML1515 — the most complete publicly available E. coli genome-scale model, with 1515 genes and ~2712 reactions. It was selected after screening 58 E. coli models from the BiGG database: iML1515 matched 11 EPS-related genes, far more than any other model (next best: 4 genes).

Why can't we just "use" all the genes?
FBA only models enzymatic reactions with defined stoichiometry (reactants, products, cofactors). Transcription factors (rpoS, csrA, lsrR) control which genes are expressed — FBA has no gene expression layer, so these cannot be part of the reaction network. Their effects are captured indirectly through the conditions we simulate (e.g. nitrogen limitation mimics the rpoS stress-response state).

3. Why the Original Model Couldn't Make EPS

Even though wcaJ (UDPGPT) and cpsG (PMANM) are in iML1515, the model could not produce EPS because:

  1. Dead-end metabolite. UDPGPT makes udcpgl_c, but nothing in iML1515 consumes it downstream — the Wca polymerisation pathway is absent. FBA forces the reaction to zero flux.
  2. No export route. For FBA to "produce" something, there must be a chain of reactions ending in an exchange reaction (EX_xxx_e). Without this, no carbon can leave the cell as EPS.
  3. c-di-GMP disconnected. ydaM (DGUNC) makes c-di-GMP, yahA/gmr degrade it — but nothing required c-di-GMP for EPS synthesis, so it cycled without effect.

Early work used a sink reaction (SK_udcpgl_c) as a proxy — just draining the precursor metabolite to allow the upstream reactions to run. This gave rough flux estimates but was not biologically meaningful as an EPS output.

4. The EPS Model v2 — What Was Built

Three new reactions were added to iML1515 to create a complete, measurable EPS output:

4a. Cellulose synthase (bcsA) — CELSYNTH

udpg_c + 0.001 cdigmp_c → cellulose_c + udp_c

UDP-glucose is the carbon donor. cdigmp_c (c-di-GMP) is included as a small stoichiometric cofactor (coefficient 0.001 per monomer) to reflect bcsA's biological requirement for c-di-GMP binding. This forces the model to run DGUNC (ydaM) to supply c-di-GMP — connecting the regulatory signal to EPS biosynthesis.

4b. Cellulose transport — CELLULOSEt

cellulose_c → cellulose_e

Moves cellulose from cytoplasm to extracellular space (represents the BcsC/D secretion system, simplified to one step).

4c. Cellulose exchange — EX_cellulose_e

cellulose_e → [secreted]

This exchange reaction is the measured EPS output. Its flux in mmol/gDW/h is what we read as the EPS production rate.

Sanity check passed: with the objective set to maximise EX_cellulose_e (no growth constraint), the model produces 8.93 mmol/gDW/h of EPS, with DGUNC (ydaM) running at 0.0089 mmol/gDW/h to supply c-di-GMP. The pathway is connected end-to-end.

4d. How the EPS phenotype is simulated

To simulate a cell in biofilm/EPS mode:

  1. Knock out CDGUNPD + LDGUNPD (yahA and gmr reactions set to zero flux). c-di-GMP degradation is blocked, so all c-di-GMP made by DGUNC must flow through CELSYNTH. This mimics the high-c-di-GMP switch that triggers biofilm formation.
  2. Two-stage optimisation: first maximise biomass to find max growth rate (μ_max), then constrain growth ≥ 90% × μ_max and maximise EX_cellulose_e flux. This gives EPS production for a growing cell, not one that has stopped dividing.

Figure 1. Growth rate, EPS flux, and per-cell EPS across 6 conditions. Combined_EPS condition produces 18× more EPS per cell than baseline.

Key biological insights from the numbers

  • Nitrogen limitation is the biggest single driver. N-limited alone gives 7.25 mmol/gDW/h — eight times baseline. When nitrogen is scarce the cell cannot build protein, so excess carbon flows into EPS instead.
  • c-di-GMP signal alone is not enough. EPS_phenotype (only yahA/gmr KO, normal nutrients) gives exactly the same EPS as baseline (0.90). The biofilm switch being "on" doesn't help if there's no carbon surplus to redirect.
  • Combined condition is most effective: high glucose (extra carbon) + N-limited (can't use carbon for protein) + c-di-GMP signal locked on = 16.44 mmol/gDW/h, ~746 fg EPS/cell/h.
  • Low oxygen modestly increases EPS but limits growth significantly (0.35 h⁻¹ vs 0.88 h⁻¹).

6. Production Envelope — Growth vs EPS Trade-off

The production envelope shows the complete Pareto frontier: at every possible growth rate, what is the maximum EPS the cell can simultaneously produce? The shaded region is the feasible space — any point inside it is theoretically achievable.

Figure 2. EPS vs biomass production envelope for 5 conditions. The combined_EPS condition (blue) has the highest EPS capacity at all growth rates. The trade-off is clear: as growth rate increases, maximum EPS decreases.

How to read this plot:
The x-axis is growth rate (h⁻¹). The y-axis is EPS production rate (mmol/gDW/h). Each curve shows the maximum EPS achievable at a given growth rate for that condition. The dashed grey line marks the baseline (unconstrained) growth rate of 0.877 h⁻¹. To maximise EPS, you move left (slower growth) and up (more EPS). The trade-off is unavoidable — both EPS and biomass compete for the same UDP-glucose pool.

8. How to Convert Model Outputs to Experimental Units

GEM outputs are in mmol/gDW/h. To compare to lab measurements:

ConversionValue
E. coli dry weight per cell~280 fg = 2.8 × 10⁻¹³ g
Cellulose monomer MW (anhydroglucose)162.14 g/mol
Formula: EPS (fg/cell/h)flux (mmol/gDW/h) × 2.8×10⁻¹³ × 162.14 × 10¹⁵

Example: combined_EPS condition = 16.44 mmol/gDW/h → 746,311 fg cellulose / cell / h = ~746 pg/cell/h = ~0.75 ng EPS per cell per hour.

Important caveat: These are maximum theoretical predictions under the given nutrient constraints. Real cells will produce less due to regulatory delays, energy overhead, and other constraints not captured in FBA. Treat these as upper bounds, not expected values. They are most useful for comparing conditions relative to each other.

10. Next Steps

  1. Run the new cells (28–32) in the notebook top-to-bottom to regenerate all v2 results. Make sure to run from cell 29 in one session — cells depend on each other.
  2. Gene knockout screen: systematically knock out each EPS-related gene one at a time and measure the effect on EPS flux. This identifies which genes are rate-limiting.
  3. Validate against literature: find published E. coli EPS production rates under N-limitation or high-glucose conditions and compare to model predictions. The combined_EPS condition (~0.75 ng/cell/h) is a starting point.
  4. Experimental design: use the condition analysis to design media for lab experiments — combined_EPS (high glucose + low ammonium) is the strongest predicted EPS-promoting condition.
  5. Consider adding the Wca pathway (colanic acid) as an alternative to cellulose. wcaJ (UDPGPT) is already in the model — adding the downstream wca steps would give a more complete picture of EPS capacity.
